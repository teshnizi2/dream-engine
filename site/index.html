About

What you are looking at

Both panels get the same keypresses. REAL is a tiny raycaster, ordinary game code on a 16×16 map. DREAM contains no game code at all: a neural network looks at the last four frames it produced and the last four actions, and imagines the next frame. Then it feeds that frame back in. After the first four frames (copied from the real game), everything you see in the DREAM panel is imagination.

How it works

The network is a small U-Net (see the parameter count above) trained as an EDM diffusion model (Karras et al.), in the way DIAMOND did for Atari: it denoises a noisy copy of the next frame, conditioned on the previous frames (stacked as extra channels) and on the actions (through adaptive group norm). The context frames are also noised during training (the trick from GameNGen), so the model can learn to repair its own mistakes; in my ablations at this scale it made no measurable difference to drift. Sampling needs very few steps: one denoising step was as accurate as three, which is why the steps buttons exist. onnxruntime-web executes the exported graph on your GPU through WebGPU, with a WASM fallback.

The training data was never recorded: the game is cheap enough to simulate, so every training batch was freshly generated from a random-walk policy (sticky random actions, wall avoidance, random respawns).

How it differs from GameNGen and Genie

GameNGen turned Doom into a neural engine by fine-tuning a Stable Diffusion model on huge amounts of recorded agent play; Genie learned playable worlds from internet video with no action labels at all. Both are far bigger systems trained on far more compute. This one is a toy on purpose: a few million parameters, 64×64 pixels, one fixed map, trained for hours, not weeks. It shows the recipe, not the state of the art.

Honest limits

  • One map only. It was trained and evaluated on this single maze. Nothing was tested on other levels, so do not expect it to generalise.
  • It drifts. Errors compound frame after frame. The drift panel shows the PSNR against the real game; “Wake up” re-seeds it from the real game.
  • Fine detail wobbles. Thin stripes (the window gallery) and brick lines are the first things to blur or flicker.
  • Human play was not tested. It was trained on exploration-like random movement. A full 360 degree spin does not bring it back to the starting view (no map memory), and long spins or hugging walls were not evaluated.
  • Speed is whatever your GPU gives. The ms/frame and fps readouts are measured live in this tab.
